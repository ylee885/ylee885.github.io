Research area 03

Visual Perception & Organization

How is visual information discriminated, integrated, and organized into objects and surfaces? How do timing demands and the structure of a stimulus change what we perceive?

Presented at VSS 2026

Simultaneous vs. sequential visual discrimination

Fine visual discrimination tracks higher-order cognition. Does it draw on different abilities when two stimuli are visible together than when one must be held in memory?

Approach

Participants judged which of two lines was longer, shown together on one screen, or one after the other on two separate screens. Latent-variable models related discrimination thresholds to working memory capacity, fluid intelligence, and attention control.

Methods & tools

  • Behavioral experiments
  • Structural equation modeling
  • R
  • Task programmed by Jason Tsukahara

Findings

Working memory capacity predicted both formats, while fluid intelligence predicted only simultaneous discrimination. Attention control predicted both but added little beyond the other two. The presentation format itself seems to shape which abilities matter.

  • VSS 2026 poster: When the wait matters: Differences in cognitive ability contributions to simultaneous vs. sequential visual discrimination. Lee, Tsukahara, & Engle.
Published 2019

Perceptual organization and kinetic occlusion

When a moving object passes in front of and behind another, do its salient parts decide whether we see it as split?

Approach

An object moved across a stationary one so that its top half was behind and its bottom half in front, an ambiguous event that can be seen as one object cut in two. I varied the object’s contour with concave or convex cusps and measured how often it looked split.

Methods & tools

  • Behavioral experiments
  • Stimulus design
  • Experiment programming
  • MATLAB
  • Paradigm and study proposed by S. H. Kim, my undergraduate advisor

Findings

Objects with concave cusps were more likely to be seen as split than objects with no cusps or convex cusps, and the more concave the contour, the more often it looked cut. The relative width of the object made no significant difference. Salient parts can be perceived as physically broken apart, which changes how we read a motion event and its causes.

  • Kim, S. H., & Lee, Y. (2019). Perception of a surface split induced by globally inconsistent kinetic occlusion: Objects with salient parts break apart easily. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 148(3), 595.
